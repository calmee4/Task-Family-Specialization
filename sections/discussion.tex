\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What Should Be Reported}

Our evidence supports three reporting recommendations for repo-grounded agent
benchmarks.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.25em]
\item Report the benchmark's official \processmetric and \resultmetric fields
separately whenever both are available, so readers can distinguish
\processmetric$=$False from \processmetric$=$True,
\resultmetric$=$False outcomes.
\item Report family or domain slices in addition to one global total.
\item Include representative official \processmetric$=$True,
\resultmetric$=$False comments for structured extraction tasks, not only
aggregate pass counts.
\end{enumerate}

These recommendations are conservative: evaluators should expose existing
official structure rather than hide it behind one number.

\subsection{A Minimal Reporting Standard}

The paper's practical recommendation can be made more concrete. A minimal
repo-grounded benchmark table should contain at least four pieces of
information. First, one row of global official totals remains useful, because it
provides a compact benchmark headline. Second, the same table or a companion
figure should report family or domain slices, so the reader can see where the
success mass actually lands. Third, the paper should expose at least a small
sample of official \processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False comments from
the families that dominate that regime. Fourth, any separate targeted slice,
such as our provider screen, should be labeled explicitly as a robustness check
rather than silently merged into the main benchmark cohort.

This standard is intentionally lightweight. It does not require trajectory
annotation, human judges, or new benchmark infrastructure. It only requires
authors to preserve and report structure that the benchmark already emits. That
is why we view the proposal as realistic. It is closer to better bookkeeping
than to a new evaluation methodology.

\subsection{Benchmark-Design Implications}

The reporting claim also has a benchmark-design implication. If a benchmark
provides family labels, evaluator comments, and intermediate states such as
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False, then future releases should treat
those fields as first-class outputs rather than as debugging residue. The same
benchmark can still keep one compact leaderboard score, but the artifact bundle
should make it easy for papers to recover family slices and the clustered
graded-but-unsuccessful regime without manual relabeling. This is consistent
with broader evaluation frameworks such as \texttt{HELM} and
\texttt{Dynabench}, which argue that benchmark reports should preserve richer
structure than one scalar headline \cite{helm,dynabench}.

Our results also suggest a practical split between benchmark-wide evaluation and
targeted stress slices. Benchmark-wide cohorts remain the only defensible basis
for claims about family concentration and robustness. Targeted slices are still
valuable, but they should be framed as probes: they can show that a family
activates at all, that a zero is not absolute, or that one model class reaches a
different intermediate regime. What they should not do is silently replace the
benchmark-wide cohort. That distinction is especially important for
repo-grounded agents, where individual families can differ sharply in external
dependencies, artifact formats, and evaluator thresholds.

\subsection{Providers Are Not One Control Condition}

The targeted provider screen adds one more practical lesson. Papers often
use one strong provider model as a qualitative control and then discuss ``closed
models'' as if they formed one stable reference class. Our local evidence does
not support that shortcut. On the same targeted GitTaskBench slice, \texttt{gpt-5-chat},
\texttt{gpt-5.2}, and \texttt{Gemini-2.5-Pro} occupy very different parts of the
\processmetric/\resultmetric space. This does not prove a frontier ranking, but
it does show that provider breadth matters even for a conservative measurement
note. One provider model can light up \texttt{Scrapy} or \texttt{NeuroKit}
while another barely reaches grading outside \texttt{PDFPlumber}.

That observation interacts directly with how open-model comparisons are read.
When an open-model cohort records zero official \resultmetric success on some
family, the next question should not be ``can one cherry-picked provider model
solve it?'' The more useful question is whether the family activates
consistently across a broader model class. In our local evidence,
\texttt{NeuroKit} and \texttt{Scrapy} move under both GPT-family models, while
image and video stressors stay dark even after the provider expansion. This is
exactly why we recommend reporting family slices rather than only one total or
one anecdotal frontier run.

\subsection{Targeted Screens Need Full-Benchmark Backstops}

The separate full-benchmark provider sweeps add a second lesson. A
targeted screen is good at finding activation points, but it can be optimistic
about how stable those activations are once the full benchmark distribution is
restored. The three full sweeps do not collapse into one provider story.
\texttt{gpt-5.2} broadens dramatically on the full benchmark, adding speech,
physiological-signal, security, and image families that are dark or only
partially active for \texttt{gpt-5-chat}, while \texttt{Gemini-2.5-Pro} stays
narrow and mostly office/security. Even within the GPT family, the full sweeps
redistribute success across families: \texttt{gpt-5-chat} keeps
\texttt{Trafilatura} as an active web family, whereas \texttt{gpt-5.2} trades
that family for broader speech and image coverage.

This matters for future method papers. If authors use a short targeted slice to
motivate a broad capability claim, they may accidentally report family
activation as if it were family robustness, or they may miss where a model's
full-benchmark success budget actually lands. Our recommendation is therefore
two-stage. Targeted screens are useful for triage, sanity checks, and
expanding model coverage quickly. But any family-level positive result that
becomes part of the paper's main narrative should be backstopped by at least
one full-benchmark or full-family check under the same official metric
contract.

\subsection{Why This Matters For Future Agent Papers}

Many future agent papers will not present a new benchmark. They will present a
new scaffold, a new planning module, a new retrieval policy, or a new model
family on an existing benchmark. In that setting, reporting only one final
success rate creates a strong temptation to over-read small improvements. An
apparent gain may come from improving just one structured family, from making
more traces gradeable without improving their final correctness, or from moving
one domain while leaving other domains unchanged.

Our measurement note does not resolve those ambiguities. It makes them visible.
That is the right scope for the current evidence. The benchmark already tells us
more than most papers currently report, and that extra information becomes more
important as agent papers become more incremental. Once multiple papers begin to
cluster around small absolute gains, family-level slices and the
\processmetric/\resultmetric split will be necessary for knowing whether two
systems improved in the same way at all.

\subsection{Working Hypotheses Behind The Family Patterns}

The current paper is descriptive, but the observed patterns already suggest
testable mechanisms. The clustered \processmetric$=$True,
\resultmetric$=$False regime is concentrated in families with schema-sensitive
or threshold-sensitive grading. \texttt{Eparse}, \texttt{PDFPlumber},
\texttt{Scrapy}, and \texttt{NeuroKit} all require the agent to produce
structured outputs that survive parsing and field-level validation after the
trace has already reached grading. The benchmark comments are consistent with
that picture: threshold misses, XML parsing failures, and evaluation-side
record-processing errors all preserve enough execution to become gradeable while
still failing the task criterion.

A second working hypothesis is that family specialization reflects different
mixtures of prior tool familiarity, data exposure, and output-format control
rather than one scalar notion of ``agent strength.'' The leading Qwen models do
not spend their success budget on the same families, and the provider screen
changes which web and physiological-signal families activate without rescuing
the image/video stressors. A plausible interpretation is that structured
extraction, light web processing, and speech JSON tasks reward different priors,
while image/video families remain bottlenecked by heavier external dependencies
and repo-specific execution demands under the current agent setup. We do not
test these mechanisms here, but they define concrete targets for the next stage
of trajectory-level analysis.

\subsection{Limitations}

The paper has clear limits. First, the main evidence is local-run rather than a
synchronized scrape of the public \gittaskbench leaderboard. Second, broader
frontier API coverage currently exists only on the small shared
\texttt{Trafilatura} slice, where we keep the latest local run per model-task
pair rather than a synchronized same-day rerun. Third, some
\processmetric$=$False mass may still reflect local harness brittleness rather
than pure model weakness. Some \processmetric$=$True,
\resultmetric$=$False rows also include evaluation-side errors rather than pure
threshold misses. For these reasons, we present the paper as a measurement note,
not as a universal scaling law.

\subsection{How This Connects to Richer Agent Analysis}

The result here is deliberately simpler than full trajectory analysis. Recent
work such as \texttt{AgentRx} and \texttt{TRAJEVAL} asks why an agent failed at a
fine-grained trajectory level \cite{agentrx,trajeval}. Our claim comes earlier
in the analysis pipeline. Before manual trajectory diagnosis, the official
benchmark outputs already justify more structured reporting. That makes the
\processmetric/\resultmetric split a useful bridge: richer trajectory work can
build on it, but we should not ignore the information already provided by the
benchmark.
