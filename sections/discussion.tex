\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Reporting Recommendations}

Our evidence supports a lightweight reporting standard for repo-grounded agent
benchmarks. A minimal benchmark table should contain four pieces of
information:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.25em]
\item A row of global official totals, providing a compact headline.
\item Family or domain slices, so readers can see where success mass
actually lands.
\item The benchmark's official \processmetric and \resultmetric fields reported
separately, so readers can distinguish \processmetric$=$False from
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False outcomes.
\item Representative official \processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False
comments from the families that dominate that regime, not only aggregate pass
counts.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Any separate targeted subset, such as our provider cohort, should be
labeled explicitly as a supplementary analysis rather than silently merged into
the main cohort. This standard requires no trajectory annotation, human judges,
or new benchmark infrastructure---only that authors preserve structure the
benchmark already emits.

\subsection{Benchmark-Design Implications}

If a benchmark provides family labels, evaluator comments, and intermediate
states such as \processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False, then future
releases should treat those fields as first-class outputs rather than as
debugging residue. The same benchmark can still keep one compact leaderboard
score, but the artifact bundle should make it easy for downstream papers to
recover family slices and the graded-but-unsuccessful regime without manual
relabeling. This is consistent with broader evaluation frameworks such as
\texttt{HELM} and \texttt{Dynabench}, which argue that benchmark reports should
preserve richer structure than one scalar headline \cite{helm,dynabench}.

\subsection{Provider Models and Targeted Evaluations}

Two practical lessons emerge from the provider cohorts. First, papers that use
one strong provider model as a qualitative control often discuss ``closed
models'' as if they formed one stable reference class. Our results show
otherwise: on the same benchmark tasks, different provider models activate
different task families. Reporting family slices is therefore important not
only for open-model comparisons but also for provider-model baselines.

Second, targeted evaluation subsets are useful for triage and for checking
whether a family activates at all, but they should not be treated as proxies
for full-benchmark evaluation. Our full-benchmark sweeps show that targeted
subsets can both overstate activations that disappear under the full task
distribution and miss activations that only emerge there. Any family-level
positive result that enters the paper's main narrative should be backstopped by
at least one full-benchmark check under the same official metric contract.

\subsection{Why This Matters For Future Agent Papers}

Many future agent papers will not present a new benchmark. They will present a
new scaffold, a new planning module, a new retrieval policy, or a new model
family on an existing benchmark. In that setting, reporting only one final
success rate creates a strong temptation to over-read small improvements. An
apparent gain may come from improving just one structured family, from making
more traces gradeable without improving their final correctness, or from moving
one domain while leaving other domains unchanged.

This paper does not resolve those ambiguities. It makes them visible. The
benchmark already tells us more than most papers currently report, and that
extra information becomes more important as agent papers become more
incremental. Once multiple papers cluster around small absolute gains,
family-level slices and the \processmetric/\resultmetric split will be
necessary for knowing whether two systems improved in the same way at all.

\subsection{Working Hypotheses}

The current paper is descriptive, but the observed patterns suggest testable
mechanisms. The clustered \processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False regime
is concentrated in families with schema-sensitive or threshold-sensitive
grading. \texttt{Eparse}, \texttt{PDFPlumber}, \texttt{Scrapy}, and
\texttt{NeuroKit} all require the agent to produce structured outputs that
survive parsing and field-level validation after the trace has already reached
grading. The benchmark comments are consistent with that picture: threshold
misses, XML parsing failures, and evaluation-side record-processing errors all
preserve enough execution to become gradeable while still failing the task
criterion.

A second hypothesis is that family specialization reflects different mixtures of
prior tool familiarity, data exposure, and output-format control rather than one
scalar notion of ``agent strength.'' The leading Qwen models do not spend their
success budget on the same families, and the provider cohort changes which web
and physiological-signal families activate without rescuing the image/video
stressors. A plausible interpretation is that structured extraction, light web
processing, and speech JSON tasks reward different priors, while image/video
families remain bottlenecked by heavier external dependencies and repo-specific
execution demands. These hypotheses define concrete targets for future
trajectory-level analysis.

\subsection{Limitations}

The paper has clear limits. Most importantly, the entire study is conducted on
a single benchmark. We chose \gittaskbench because it is, to our knowledge, the
only repository-grounded agent benchmark that emits a structured
\processmetric/\resultmetric split as part of its official evaluator contract,
making the analysis possible without inventing new metrics. However, the
specific patterns we observe---the domain skew, the family specialization, the
clustering of the graded-but-unsuccessful regime---may be partly shaped by how
\gittaskbench is constructed and weighted. Validating these structural
observations on other benchmarks (e.g., \texttt{SWE-bench}, \texttt{OSWorld})
would strengthen the generalizability of the reporting recommendations,
although those benchmarks would first need to expose comparable intermediate
evaluation states.

Beyond the single-benchmark scope, several additional limits apply. The main
evidence is a fixed study cohort rather than a synchronized scrape of the
public \gittaskbench leaderboard. Broader provider coverage exists only on the
shared \texttt{Trafilatura} subset and the targeted provider cohort, neither of
which is a matched frontier rerun. The supplementary open-model breadth cohort
is task-limited. Some \processmetric$=$False mass may reflect
evaluation-pipeline brittleness rather than pure model weakness, and some
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False rows include evaluation-side
errors rather than threshold misses. Finally, each cohort total is a single-run
count rather than a variance estimate. Although generation uses temperature
\texttt{0.0}, minor API-level non-determinism cannot be fully excluded, so
small cell-level differences in the task heatmap should be read descriptively
rather than as stable rankings.
