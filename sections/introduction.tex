\section{Introduction}

Agent evaluation has expanded rapidly from single-turn QA into web navigation,
mobile environments, software engineering, and repository-grounded tool use
\cite{agentbench,webarena,visualwebarena,androidworld,swebench,swelancer,ambigswe,longsoftwaretasks,gittaskbench,toolgym,deepresearchbench,mcpbench,mcpuniverse}.
Repository-grounded benchmarks are especially demanding: they require agents to
interact with real codebases, produce structured artifacts, and pass evaluator
scripts that can distinguish partial execution from full task completion. Yet
the dominant reporting convention collapses all of this into a single global
success rate per model.

That single number discards structure the benchmark has already computed. Prior
work has identified this concern from multiple angles: trajectory-level
diagnosis reveals that final success hides execution-level failure modes
\cite{agentrx,trajeval}; long-context measurement shows that aggregate scores
can conceal task-specific variation
\cite{lostmiddle,longbench,ruler,infinitebench,nolima,loogle,illusiondiminishingreturns};
and multimodal evaluation demonstrates that answer channel and benchmark family
affect what a score actually means
\cite{mme,mmmu,mathvista,hallusionbench,blink}. These critiques converge on a
shared lesson: when a benchmark already records intermediate evaluation states,
collapsing them into one headline number throws away evidence the evaluator has
already provided.

\gittaskbench \cite{gittaskbench} provides exactly this kind of underused
structure. Its official evaluator emits two fields for every graded trace:
\processmetric, which marks whether the trace advanced far enough to produce a
gradeable artifact, and \resultmetric, which marks whether that artifact
satisfies the task criterion. This creates three observable states---traces that
never reach grading, traces that reach grading but fail, and traces that fully
succeed---yet published results typically report only the last.

We audit this official \processmetric/\resultmetric split across a comparable
open-model cohort evaluated on the full \gittaskbench task set, and supplement
it with provider-model evaluations at two granularities---a targeted task screen
and full-benchmark sweeps---so that the analysis does not rest on a single model
family. All reported numbers are aggregated from the benchmark's official
outputs; derived rates and visualizations are marked as paper-side summaries.

Four patterns emerge from this audit. First, official \resultmetric success is
narrow and domain-skewed, concentrating in office-document, security, web, and
speech families while image and video families produce no observed success.
Second, models that tie on global \resultmetric totals succeed on materially
different task families, revealing hidden specialization that a single score
cannot surface. Third, provider models do not form one uniform control
condition: they activate different families on the same benchmark tasks, and
targeted screens can both overstate and miss activations found in full-benchmark
sweeps. Fourth, the intermediate \processmetric$=$True,
\resultmetric$=$False regime clusters in structured-extraction families rather
than appearing as random residue, mixing threshold misses, parser failures, and
evaluation-side errors.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.25em]
\item We show that official \resultmetric success on \gittaskbench is highly
concentrated in a small subset of domains and task families rather than being
smoothly distributed across the benchmark.
\item We show that tied global \resultmetric totals hide material task-family
specialization across models.
\item We show that provider models are not interchangeable on \gittaskbench:
they activate different task families, and targeted evaluation slices do not
reliably predict full-benchmark outcomes.
\item We identify a structured \processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False
regime that clusters in extraction-heavy families and should be reported
explicitly in future repo-grounded evaluations.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent This paper is a descriptive benchmark study. We report exact observed
counts from the evaluated cohorts and use provider slices as robustness checks
rather than as frontier rankings.
