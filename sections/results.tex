\section{Results}

\subsection{Global Totals Are Narrow and Domain-Skewed}

Table~\ref{tab:model-summary} gives the exact official totals for the comparable
local cohort. The raw totals are already modest: several leading models tie at
the top, while three models remain at zero on both official fields.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} adds the broader distributional picture. Official
success is not spread evenly across the benchmark. In the comparable local
cohort, office-document and security families contribute most of the observed
\resultmetric success, web and speech contribute a smaller residue,
physiological signals reach grading without \resultmetric success, and image
and video are complete zeros.

This distribution matters because a single global score can suggest a smooth
ordering that the domain slices do not support. In our cohort, different
families occupy different parts of the official \processmetric/\resultmetric
space, and some families currently produce no observed official success at all.
Table~\ref{tab:domain-summary} makes the imbalance explicit. Office and security
do not merely have higher absolute success counts; they also occupy most of the
observed \processmetric-true mass. By contrast, image and video are complete
zeros under both official fields in this cohort. This means a single benchmark
score can blur together at least three qualitatively different states: domains
with repeatable task completion, domains with gradeable-but-unsuccessful traces,
and domains with no observed official traction at all.

This point becomes stronger once we separate benchmark mass from success mass.
Image tasks constitute the single largest domain block in the comparable local
cohort, but
they contribute nothing to observed official success. Office and security are
much smaller by trace count than image plus video combined, yet they account for
nearly all observed \resultmetric success. A single total cannot
tell the reader whether a model is weak everywhere, strong only on structured
extraction, or simply facing a benchmark where most active success mass lives in
just a few families.

\subsection{Tied Headline Scores Hide Family Specialization}

The three headline leaders in the comparable local cohort tie on official
\resultmetric success, but the tied total is misleading. \texttt{Qwen2.5-14B}
reaches official success in \texttt{Eparse}, \texttt{PDFPlumber}, and
\texttt{Faker}. \texttt{Qwen2.5-32B} keeps those families and adds
\texttt{SpeechBrain}. \texttt{Qwen3-32B} swaps \texttt{Eparse} for
\texttt{Trafilatura}. Figure~\ref{fig:task-heatmap} makes this visible at task
level. \texttt{Eparse\_02}, \texttt{Faker\_03}, and
\texttt{PDFPlumber\_03} are repeatable successes across multiple models, but
other wins are singleton or near-singleton events such as
\texttt{Trafilatura\_01} for \texttt{Qwen3-32B} and \texttt{Trafilatura\_03}
for \texttt{Mistral-Small-24B}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/figure_task_heatmap.pdf}
    \caption{Official \resultmetric task heatmap for all tasks with at least one observed success in the comparable local cohort. Several models tie globally, but they do not win on the same task set.}
    \label{fig:task-heatmap}

This is the paper's clearest measurement point. A single global success rate can
hide material specialization even before we look at trajectories, prompts, or
manual annotations. If the reporting goal is to understand what a repo-grounded
agent can actually do, the family slice matters.

It also matters for how we read ties. A shared top-line total is easy to over-interpret
as evidence that the three models are functionally interchangeable. The task
heatmap shows that they are not. One model's official success budget is spent on
\texttt{Eparse}; another's is spent on \texttt{Trafilatura}; another picks up
\texttt{SpeechBrain}. These are not cosmetic differences. They imply different
failure surfaces, different benchmark coverage, and different places where a
future method paper might appear to ``improve the agent'' simply by improving
one narrow family.

\subsection{The Local Evidence Already Extends Beyond Qwen}

The comparable local cohort contains only one non-Qwen model with non-zero
official \resultmetric success: \texttt{Mistral-Small-24B}. Its wins are \texttt{Eparse\_02} and
\texttt{Trafilatura\_03}. That is enough to prevent the main observation from
reducing to a purely within-Qwen comparison, although same-task non-Qwen
coverage remains limited. The shared-task \texttt{Trafilatura} slice extends
the point further. Table~\ref{tab:shared-trafilatura} shows that
\texttt{gpt-5.2} and \texttt{Gemini-2.5-Pro} both achieve official
\processmetric$=$1, \resultmetric$=$1 on \texttt{Trafilatura\_02}. Meanwhile,
\texttt{Qwen3-32B} is the only model in the local roster that reaches official
success on \texttt{Trafilatura\_01}.

\input{tables/shared_trafilatura}

The shared-task slice is small and not synchronized as a frontier-wide rerun, so
we do not claim a stable frontier ranking. The supported claim is narrower:
once the same official tasks are aligned, official non-Qwen successes already
appear in local evidence.

\subsection{A Supplementary Provider Screen Changes The Active Families}

The shared \texttt{Trafilatura} slice broadens coverage across models but only on
a narrow slice. To test whether the family picture changes on a slightly wider
slice, we complete the same targeted provider screen for three provider models:
\texttt{gpt-5-chat}, \texttt{gpt-5.2}, and \texttt{Gemini-2.5-Pro}. The task set
includes office, security, speech, web, physiological-signal, image, and video
tasks, and it is scored only with official \processmetric/\resultmetric
outputs.

\input{tables/provider_screen}

The resulting spread is already substantial. \texttt{gpt-5-chat} leads the
screen on both official fields and spans the broadest family footprint.
\texttt{gpt-5.2} follows with a slightly narrower family footprint.
\texttt{Gemini-2.5-Pro} reaches official success only on
\texttt{PDFPlumber\_01}. The provider screen therefore broadens the evidence
base, but it does not behave like a single homogeneous ``closed-model'' control
condition.

The family pattern is more informative than the totals. Both GPT-family models
convert \texttt{Scrapy\_03} and \texttt{NeuroKit\_01} into official
\resultmetric success, whereas the comparable local cohort had no official
\resultmetric-positive rows at all in \texttt{Scrapy} or \texttt{NeuroKit}.
\texttt{gpt-5-chat} additionally reaches official success on
\texttt{SpeechBrain\_05}; \texttt{gpt-5.2} does not. \texttt{Gemini-2.5-Pro},
by contrast, activates only \texttt{PDFPlumber}. This is exactly the kind of
structure that a single global pass rate would obscure. If we reported only one
provider total, we would hide the fact that ``provider strength'' is itself
family-selective in local evidence.

The provider screen also changes how we should read the local zeros. A zero in
the comparable local cohort need not mean the family is inherently impossible under the
benchmark's official metric. \texttt{NeuroKit} and \texttt{Scrapy} illustrate
this clearly. In the comparable local cohort they contribute only to the
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False regime or to complete failure. In
the provider screen they become official \resultmetric-positive families for
both GPT-family models. By contrast, the image and video stressors,
\texttt{AnimeGANv3\_01} and \texttt{VideoPose3D\_01}, remain
\processmetric$=$False for all three provider models. That difference is
important: some zeros are family-specific capability gaps that move with model
class, while others look more like benchmark-wide dead zones under the current
agent setup.

The screen also shows that stronger totals do not eliminate the intermediate
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False regime. Both \texttt{gpt-5-chat}
and \texttt{gpt-5.2} still spend mass there on \texttt{PDFPlumber\_02} and
\texttt{Trafilatura\_01}. \texttt{gpt-5.2} additionally lands in that regime on
\texttt{PDFPlumber\_01}. This matters for interpretation. The stronger provider
models are not simply converting every non-success into clean official success;
they are redistributing failures across the benchmark's existing official
states.

\subsection{A Full-Benchmark Provider Run Shows The Screen Is Not Enough}

The targeted provider screen is useful for broadening model coverage, but it is
still a targeted slice. To test how far that slice extrapolates, we run
\texttt{gpt-5-chat} on the full benchmark. Table~\ref{tab:provider-full54-domain}
shows that the resulting successes are again strongly concentrated. Office, web,
and security dominate the successful domains. By contrast, speech remains dark,
physiological signals stay in the graded-but-unsuccessful regime, image only
activates narrowly, and video stays dark.

\input{tables/provider_full54_domain}

The family breakdown explains why the full sweep is more informative than the
screen alone. Several screen-positive families remain robust when the full
family is restored: \texttt{PDFPlumber}, \texttt{PyPDF2}, \texttt{Scrapy}, and
\texttt{Faker} are all perfect official-success families for
\texttt{gpt-5-chat} on this run, and \texttt{Trafilatura} retains one official
success on \texttt{Trafilatura\_03}. But other screen positives do not
generalize uniformly. \texttt{NeuroKit\_01} was an official success in the
targeted provider screen, yet the full-benchmark run yields
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False on \texttt{NeuroKit\_01} and
\texttt{NeuroKit\_02}, followed by a complete failure on
\texttt{NeuroKit\_03}. The image side remains even narrower: the only official
\resultmetric-positive image task is \texttt{SuperResolution\_03}; the rest of
the image/video slice remains unsuccessful.

This is the clearest reason not to treat a targeted provider slice as a hidden
replacement leaderboard. The screen correctly shows that some local zeros are
not benchmark-wide impossibilities: provider models can light up
\texttt{Scrapy}, \texttt{NeuroKit}, and broader office families. But the
full-benchmark provider run shows that family activation and family robustness are
not the same claim. A short screen can reveal that a family is reachable at
all, while still overstating how consistently that reachability extends across
the benchmark's full task distribution.

\subsection{The \processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False Regime Clusters}

The comparable local cohort contains a visible band of official
\processmetric$=$True and \resultmetric$=$False outcomes. These rows are not
spread evenly across the benchmark. They cluster in \texttt{Eparse},
\texttt{PDFPlumber}, \texttt{Scrapy}, and \texttt{NeuroKit}. The benchmark's
official comments show that this regime is heterogeneous. On
\texttt{PDFPlumber\_02}, multiple models reach grading but miss the required
$75\%$ cell-level threshold, e.g., $64.92\%$ for
\texttt{Qwen2.5-14B}. On \texttt{Scrapy\_03}, the official comment
reports XML parsing failure. On \texttt{Eparse\_03}, one official comment
reports an evaluation-side error after grading begins.

\input{tables/process_gap_examples}

We therefore do not interpret every \processmetric$=$True,
\resultmetric$=$False row as the same failure mode. The narrower point is that
collapsing these rows into generic total failure erases a benchmark-visible
intermediate regime. That regime is already concentrated enough to reveal where
additional reporting detail is most needed. Table~\ref{tab:process-gap-examples}
shows four representative cases from different families. Two patterns are
especially important. First, not all graded-but-unsuccessful rows are equal:
some are close threshold misses such as \texttt{PDFPlumber\_02}, while others
are schema or parser breakdowns. Second, official comments already separate
these cases without any new judge or taxonomy. That makes the regime useful for
benchmark reporting even before richer trajectory-level diagnosis.

This intermediate regime is also where future benchmark analysis can gain the
most leverage. A method paper that converts \texttt{PDFPlumber\_02} from
\processmetric$=$True, \resultmetric$=$False into full success is doing
something different from a method paper that merely converts
\processmetric$=$False into \processmetric$=$True on image tasks. Both changes
may help the final success rate, but they act on different parts of the failure
surface. Reporting only one global number prevents that distinction.

\subsection{Interpreting The Measurement Claim}

The combined picture is now sharper. Global official totals remain useful for a
headline snapshot, but they are too lossy to stand alone. Domain slices show
where success mass is concentrated. Task-level heatmaps show that tied totals do
not imply the same competence profile. The graded-but-unsuccessful regime shows
that even within failure, some traces are substantially more informative than
others. These are modest claims, but they are operationally important. A paper
that reports only one \resultmetric total discards benchmark-visible structure
that already exists in official outputs.
